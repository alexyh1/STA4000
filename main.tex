\begin{abstract}
\begin{spacing}{1.5}
\normalsize
\bigskip
Optimal transport provides a mathematical framework for computing the
most cost-efficient way of transporting one probability distribution of mass
into another. In recent years, it has found widespread applications in computer science, physics, statistics, economics, cell biology and other fields.
Topics include: Monge and Kantorovich formulation, Wasserstein distances,
c-cyclical monotonicity, dual problems, entropic regularization, statistical
convergence, gradient flows, generalized optimal transport problems (Weak
OT, unbalanced OT, Wasserstein barycenters, etc). Computational approaches and numerical algorithms will also be introduced. Weekly discussions involve literature reviews on recent research papers and presenting derivations / code implementations from the textbook(s). While these notes closely follow the main text of ”Optimal Transport for Machine Learners” by Gabriel Peyré, I will try to make it instructional by including my own examples / explanations, data exercises and results from literature reviews. Code implementations for all figures are available at \url{https://github.com/alexyh1/STA4000}.
\newline
\newline
Pre/Co-Requisites: Graduate Real Analysis, Graduate Probability.
\newline
Supplemental Readings: Statistical Optimal Transport by Chewi et al, Topics in Optimal Transportation by Villani.

\end{spacing}

\section{Monge Formulation}

We will first proceed with some prerequisite knowledge for formulating the Optimal Transport problem.

\subsection{Measures}
\begin{definition}[Probability Simplex]
    The probability simplex of length $n$ is defined by $\Sigma_n = \{a \in \mathbb{R}^n_{\geq 0}: \sum_{i = 1}^n a_i = 1\}$.
    \newline
    Elements of the $\Sigma_n$ are commonly called probability vectors.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Discrete Measure]
A discrete measure with weights $a$ $x_1, ..., x_n \in X$ is defined by $\mu = \sum_{i = 1}^n a_i \delta_{x_i}$ where $\delta_{x_i}$ is the Dirac measure located at $x_i \in \mathbb{R}$.
\newline
$\mu$ is a probability measure if $a \in \Sigma_n$ ($\mu: X \rightarrow [0, \infty]$ such that $\mu(X) = 1$, monotonicity, sub-additivity and $\sigma$-additivity hold).
\end{definition}

We require sufficient regularity conditions for many of the results in the book; unless otherwise mentioned, we will be working with positive Borel measures $\mu \in \mathcal{M}(X)$ on a topological metric space $(X, d)$ where $\mu$ is defined on all Borel sets (Complements, unions, or intersections of open sets in $X$). In general, we will be working on positive probability measures denoted $\mu \in \mathcal{M}(X)$. We usually also assume that the metric space $(X, d)$ is Polish such that it is complete (Every Cauchy sequence converges) and there exists a countable dense set in $X$ (Ex. The general topology on $\mathbb{R}$ is Polish, whereas the topology induced by the discrete metric is not).

\begin{definition}[Support of Measure]
    The support of a Borel measure $\mu$ on a metric space ($X$, d) is given by $\text{supp}(\mu) = \{x \in X: \mu(B_{\varepsilon}(x)) > 0 \ \forall \varepsilon > 0\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Radon Measure]
A Radon measure $\mu$ on a metric space $X$ is a Borel measure such that $\mu(K) < \infty$ for all compact sets $K \subset X$, $\mu(E) = \inf\{\mu(U): U \supset E, U \text{ is open}\}$ for Borel sets $E$ and $\mu(A) = \sup\{\mu(B): B \subset A, B \text{ is compact}\}$.
\end{definition}

We can restrict our attention to Radon measures as every Polish space is a Radon space, such that Borel probability measures in a Polish space (Which we consider) are indeed Radon measures by definition.

\begin{proposition}[Discretization of Lebesgue Integral]
    Suppose $f$ is a measurable function ($\{x: f(x) < t\}$ are Borel sets wrt $\mu$), then 
    \[
    \langle f, \mu \rangle = \displaystyle \int_X f(x)d\mu(x) = \sum_{i = 1}^n a_i f(x_i).
    \]
\end{proposition}

The discretization follows from Riesz-Markov-Kakutani's Theorem such that integration on a finite compact space and measure admits a continuous linear functional on the Banach space of continuous functions and vice-versa.

\begin{definition}[Relative Density]
    If $\mu$ is absolutely continuous to a reference measure $\lambda$ denoted $\mu << \lambda$ ($\lambda(A) = 0 \Rightarrow \mu(A) = 0$) then the relative density is its Radon-Nikodym derivative $\rho_\mu = \frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}$, and for all continuous functions $h$ on $X$, 
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_X h(x)d\mu(x) = \displaystyle \int_X h(x) \rho_\mu(x) d\lambda(x).
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Total Variation]
    The total variation of a finite signed Radon measure $\mu$ on a compact space $X$ is given by $\lvert \mu \rvert_{TV} = \sup_{f \in \mathcal{C}(X)}\{\langle f, \mu \rangle: \lvert f \rvert _\infty \leq 1\}$
\end{definition}

It follows trivially that total variation of probability measures are always equal to 1.

\begin{definition}[Absolute Value of Measures]
    The measure-theoretic equivalent definition of total variation is given by its absolute value denoted as $\lvert \mu \rvert (A) = \sup_{A = \cup_i B_i} \sum_i \lvert \mu(B_i) \rvert$ ie finite or countable measurable partitions of $A$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
    Let $\mu$ be a measure that is absolutely continuous to the Lebesgue measure such that $d\mu = (3x -2) \mathds{1}_{[-1, 1]}dx$. Then the total variation of $\mu$ via 1.7 and 1.8 is given by 
    \[
    \lvert \mu([-1, 1]) \rvert = \lvert \mu([-1, \frac{2}{3}])| + \lvert \mu((\frac{2}{3}, 1]) \rvert,
    \]
    Then the Lebesgue integrals can be easily computed via 1.6 as
    \[
    \lvert \mu \rvert ([-1, 1]) =  \lvert \displaystyle \int_{-1}^{\frac{2}{3}} (3x-2)dx \rvert + \displaystyle \int_{\frac{2}{3}}^1 (3x-2)dx = \frac{25}{6}+\frac{1}{6} = \frac{13}{3}. \hfill \tag*{\(\square\)}
    \]
\end{example}

Notice that we can verify that the definitions of total variation are indeed equivalent via the following proposition.

\begin{proposition}[Dual Definition of Total Variation]
    For a finite signed Radon measure $\mu$ on a compact space $X$, we have $\lvert \mu \rvert_{TV} = \lvert \mu \rvert (X)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    To show the forward inequality, it follows that
    \[
    \lvert \displaystyle \int f d\mu \rvert \leq \displaystyle \int \lvert f \rvert \ d \lvert \mu \rvert \leq \lvert f \rvert_\infty \lvert \mu \rvert (X).
    \]
    For the reverse inequality, let $\mu = \mu^+ - \mu^-$ via Jordan decomposition such that $\lvert \mu \rvert = \mu^+ + \mu^-$, then $s = \frac{d\mu}{d\lvert \mu \rvert}$ = -1 or 1 excluding null sets, such that $d \mu = s \ d \lvert \mu \rvert$. By regularity of Radon measures on compact spaces we can find a sequence of continuous functions $f_k \in L^1 (\lvert \mu \rvert)$ (i.e. $\displaystyle \int \lvert f_k \rvert \ d\mu < \infty$) that approximates $s$ with $\lvert f_k \rvert_\infty \leq 1$, therefore
    \[
    \displaystyle \int f_k d\mu \rightarrow \displaystyle \int s d\mu = \lvert \mu \rvert (X) . \qedhere
    \]   
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    For continuous measures $d\mu = \rho_\mu d\lambda$ and $d\nu = \rho_\nu d\lambda$ we obtain $\lvert \mu - \nu \rvert_{TV} = \displaystyle \int_X \lvert \rho_\mu(x) - \rho_\nu(x) \rvert d\lambda(x)$, whereas the discrete formula is given by $\lvert \mu - \nu \rvert_{TV} = \sum_{k} \lvert a_k - b_k \rvert$ when $\mu = \sum_k a_k \delta_{z_k}$ and $\nu = \sum_k b_k \delta_{z_k}$ (Unionize supports and set missing coefficients to 0).
    \newline
    For probability measures, integration cleanly simplifies to $\displaystyle \int_X f(x) d\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}(f(X))$.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Push-Forwards}
\begin{definition}[Push-Forward]
    For a continuous map $T: X \rightarrow Y$, the push-forward measure is $\nu = T_\# \mu \in \mathcal{M}(Y)$ such that $\mu \in \mathcal{M}(X)$ satisfies
    \[
    \forall h \in \mathcal{C}(Y), \displaystyle \int_Y h(y)d\nu(y) = \displaystyle \int_X h(T(x))d\mu(x),
    \]
    Where $T_\#: \mathcal{M}(X) \rightarrow \mathcal{M}(Y)$ is the push-forward map.
    Equivalently, $\nu(B) = \mu(T^{-1}(B))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Discrete measures follow neatly such that for $\mu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{x_i}$, its push-forward measure is given by $\nu = \sum_i a_i \delta_{T(x_i)}$.
\end{remark}

The existence of push-forward map begs the question of whether a function can be "pulled back" into the domain of $T$ / metric space $X$, which is outlined in the definition below. 

\begin{definition}[Pull-Back]
    The pull-back by $T$ denoted $T^\#: \mathcal{C}(Y) \rightarrow \mathcal{C}(X)$ such that for a continuous function $g$, $T^\#g = g \circ T$. From Definition 1.10 we immediately obtain
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_X T^\#g(x)d\mu(x) = \displaystyle \int_Y g(y)d(T_\#\mu)(y).
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Change of Variables for Relative Densities]
    Let $\mu, \nu$ have relative densities $\rho_\mu, \rho_\nu$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$, if $T$ is a $C^1$ diffeomorphism and $T$ pushes $\mu$ to $\nu$, then
    \[
    \rho_\nu(y) = \rho_\mu(T^{-1}(y))\frac{1}{\det(T'(T^{-1}(y)))}.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Using the Radon-Nikodym formulation of the relative densities (Definition 1.6) and setting $y = T(x)$ along with change of variables we get that
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_Y h(y)\rho_\nu(y)dy = \displaystyle \int_Y h(y) d\nu(y) = \displaystyle \int_X h(T(x))d\mu(x) \]
    \[ = \displaystyle \int_X h(T(x)) \rho_\mu dx = \displaystyle \int_Y h(y) \rho_\mu(T^{-1}(y))\frac{dy}{\det(T'(T^{-1}(y)))}
    \]
    for all $h$ therefore equality of the integrands hold. By bijectivity of $T$ we get $\rho_\mu(x) = \lvert \det(T'(x))\rvert \rho_\nu(T(x))$ completing the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
    Let $\mu \sim N(0, I_2), \nu \sim N(b, a^2I_2)$ for $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $a > 0$ such that $T(x) = ax + b$ is a $C^1$ diffeomorphism and $\nu$ indeed the push-foward map of $T$. Then $T^{-1}(y) = \frac{x - b}{a}$, $T' = aI$ such that
    \[
    \rho_\nu(y) = \rho_\mu(\frac{y - b}{a}) \times \frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{(2\pi)a} \exp\{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{y - b}{a})^T I (\frac{y - b}{a})\}
    \]
    \[
    = \frac{1}{2a\pi}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}(y-b)^T (a^2I)^{-1} (y - b)\}
    \]
    Which is indeed the density function of $\nu$ and completes our sanity check. \hfill \(\square\)
\end{example}

\subsection{Monge's Problem}
\begin{definition}[Monge Problem]
For two measures $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X), \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(Y)$ and a cost function $c: X \times Y \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, Monge's problem is formulated as
\[
\mathcal{M}_c(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{T: X \rightarrow Y} \{\int_X c(x, T(x))d\mu(x), T_\#\mu = \nu\}.
\]
In other words, $T$ satisfies the push-forward constraint of $\mu$ wrt $\nu$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Monge's problem for discrete probability measures $\mu = \sum_{i = 1}^n a_i \delta_{x_i}, \nu = \sum_{j = 1}^n b_j \delta_{y_i}$ is formulated as
    \[
    \mathcal{M}_c(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{T: X \rightarrow Y} \{\sum_{i = 1}^n a_ic(x_i, T(x_i)), \sum_{i: T(x_i) = y_j} a_i = b_j\}.
    \]
\end{remark}

Notice that Monge's problem does not guarantee the existence of such a map; consider the Borel measures $\mu = \delta_1$ and $\nu = \frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1} + \frac{1}{2}\delta_2$, then there is no well-defined map $T$ that "splits" the single point into two. However, existence and uniqueness regularity conditions will be explored in section 1.4, and a more general solution will be discussed via Kantorovich. Here, we are able to show existence for atomless Borel measures on probability spaces.

\begin{proposition}[Existence of Transport Maps from Atomless Distributions]
    Let $\mu, \nu$ be Borel measures on Polish spaces and assume $\mu$ is atomless, then there exists a measurable map $T$ such that $T_\# \mu = \nu$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    It suffices to construct a map from $[0, 1]$ to $\nu$; choose a Borel isomorphism $i$ from the support of $\nu$ onto a Borel subset of $[0, 1]$, let $\beta = i_\#\nu$, and consider the inverse of the cdf of $\beta$, then this map sends Lebesgue measure on $[0, 1]$ to $\beta$ and is defined a.s. on $i(\text{supp}(\nu))$ and composing it with $i^{-1}$ and the source isomorphism completes the proof. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Monge Distance]
    Suppose $X = Y$ and $c(x, y) = d(x, y)^p$ for some $p > 0$ and metric $d$, then the Monge Distance is defined as 
    \[
    \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)^p = \inf_{T: X \rightarrow X} \{\displaystyle \displaystyle \int_X d(x, T(x))^pd\mu(x); T_\#\mu = \nu\}
    \]
    where $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p^p = \infty$ if no such infimum exists.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
    Assume $X = Y$ is a metric space, then the quantity $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p$ is non-negative, vanishes only on the diagonal and satisfies the triangle inequality.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Non-negativity follows immediately. Notice on the diagonal, $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu) = 0$ then there exists a sequence of maps $T_k$ such that 
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_X d(x, T_k(x)^pd\mu(x) \rightarrow 0.
    \]
    Then for bounded 1-Lipschitz functions $h$,
    \[
    \lvert \displaystyle \int hd\nu - \displaystyle \int h d\mu \rvert = \lvert \displaystyle \int (h(T_k(x) - h(x))d\mu(x) \rvert \leq \int d_x(T_k(x)) d\mu(x) \leq (\int d_x(T_k(x))^p d\mu(x))^p \rightarrow 0.
    \]
    Since 1-Lipschitz functions separate probability measures on metric spaces, it follows that $\mu = \nu$.
    \newline
    \newline
    The infinity case for the triangle inequality follows by contradiction such that for measures $\mu, \gamma, \nu$ with respective push-forward maps $S_\#\mu = \gamma, T_\# \gamma = \nu$ then composing $(S \circ T)_\#\mu = \nu$ contradicts $LHS = \infty$ by the infimum. Therefore for the finite case, pick $\varepsilon$ such that
    \[
    \mathcal{E}_\mu(S)^\frac{1}{p} \leq \mathcal{W}_p(\mu, \gamma) + \varepsilon, \ \ \mathcal{E}_\nu(T)^\frac{1}{p} \leq \mathcal{W}_p(\gamma, \nu)    \]
    The composed map is indeed feasible such that
    \[
    \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu) \leq (\displaystyle \int d(x, T(S(x))^pd\mu(x))^\frac{1}{p} \leq (\displaystyle \int d(x, S(x))^pd\mu(x))^\frac{1}{p} + (\displaystyle \int d(S(x), T(S(x))^pd\mu(x))^\frac{1}{p}
    \]
    \[
    = \mathcal{E}_\mu(S)^\frac{1}{p} + \mathcal{E}_\nu(T)^\frac{1}{p} \leq\mathcal{W}_p(\mu, \gamma) + \mathcal{W}_p(\gamma, \nu) + 2\varepsilon
    \]
    Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary, this completes the proof. 
\end{proof}

In many cases Monge's distance is not well-defined, as well as $\tilde{\mathcal{W}_p}$ fails to be a metric; consider the case where $\mu \sim N(0, 1)$ and $\nu = \delta_1$, then $T = 1$ such that all the mass of $\mu$ is pushed to 1 with finite cost, but $\nu$ to $\mu$ is infeasible such that $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\nu, \mu) = \infty$. Neverless, we introduce several regularity conditions for existence and uniqueness of Monge's problem.

\subsection{Regularity Conditions for Monge's Problem}

\begin{theorem}[Brenier's Theorem]
    Let $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(\mathbb{R}^d)$ have finite second moments, and assume $\mu$ is absolutely continuous wrt the Lebesgue measure. Then for quadratic $c(x, y) = \lvert x - y \rvert^2$ there exists a convex function $\psi: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}$ such that $T = \nabla \psi, \ T_\#\mu = \nu$
    where $T$ is the unique Monge map almost everywhere.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    See Villani \cite{villani} Section 3.3.
\end{proof}

It easily follows that $\psi$ is unique $\mu$-almost everywhere up to an additive constant. 

\begin{example}
    Let $\mu \sim N(0, 1)$ and $\nu \sim \text{Unif}[0, 1]$, then $T(x) = 2x + 1$ and $T_\# \mu = \nu$ with Monge distance
    \[
    \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_2(\mu, \nu)^2 = \displaystyle \int (x + 1)^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\{{-\frac{x^2}{2}\}} = 2
    \]
    And $\psi(x) = x^2 +x + C$ is convex such that $T = \nabla \psi. \hfill \(\square\)$.

\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Brenier's Polar Factorization]
Let $\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be endowed with the normalized Lebesgue measure $\lambda$, and $u \in L^2(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$; assume $v = u_\#\lambda$ has finite second moment. Then there exists a measure-preserving map $s: \Omega \rightarrow \Omega$ and convex function $\psi$ such that $u = \nabla \psi \circ s$ and $\nabla \psi$ is unique almost everywhere.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    See Santambrogio Theorem 1.53.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    Several applications of Brenier's Polar Factorization follows as the classical transportation problem records the final mass distribution but not the mapping of transported points; Polar Factorization creates identifiability such that $s$ rearranges labels but not mass, and the transformation $\nabla \psi$ is now tractable. Secondly, Theorem 1.18 can be thought of as a generalization of Polar Decomposition (Such that any real square matrix $A$ can be decomposed into $UP$ where $U$ is orthogonal and $P$ is symmetric PSD), where orthogonal matrices become measure-preserving transformations and matrices become maps.
\end{remark}

Monge's problem not only gives us the optimal transport map but also parametrizes the entire path between the two probability distributions. In fact, each point is transported at constant speed from its initial to its newly mapped position. 

\begin{definition}[McCann's Displacement Interpolation]
    If $T_\#\mu = \nu$, then the Monge interpolation generated by $T$ is given by
    \[
    T_t(x) = (1-t)x+tT(x), \ \mu_t = ({T_t})_\#\mu
    \]
    Where $t \in [0, 1]$, and for quadratic cost when $T$ is the optimal transport map, this curve is known as McCann's displacement interpolation. 
\end{definition}

For Monge formulations with a null constraint set, the displacement interpolation can be generated to Kantorovich solutions (See Section 2). Naturally, Monge's Displacement Interpolation constructs a geodesic for the set of probability measures on $\mathbb{R}^d$ wrt the directed Monge distance.

\begin{proposition}[Directed Monge Displacement Geodesics]
    Let $p \geq 1$, $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(\mathbb{R}^d)$ have finite $p$-th moments, and $T$ be the optimal transport map from 1.15. Set $\mu_t = (1-t)\text{id} + tT$. Assume $t < 1$ that $T_t$ is one-to-one on a full $\mu$-measure Borel set, such that $T_t^{-1}$ is defined a.e. Then for $0 \leq s \leq t \leq 1$,
    \[
    \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_s, \mu_t) = (t-s)\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)
    \]
    Such that $t \rightarrow \mu_t$ is an oriented constant-speed geodesic for the Monge distance.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    $s = t$ is trivial so let $t > s$. Then since $s < 1$, $T_s^{-1}$ is defined a.e. wrt $\mu_s$, then define $S_{s, t} = T_t \circ T_s^{-1}$ and $(S_{s, t})_\#\mu_s = \mu_t$ and using the optimality of $T$ we get
    \[
    \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_s, \mu_t)^p \leq \displaystyle \int \lvert S_{s, t}(z) - z \rvert^pd\mu_s(z) = \int \rvert T_t(x) - T_s(x) \rvert^p d\mu(x) = (t - s)^p \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)^p
    \]
    So immediately we get $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \mu_s) \leq s\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_t, \nu) \leq (1-t)\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)$. Then via triangle inequality of Monge
    \[
    \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu) \leq \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \mu_s) + \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_s, \mu_t) + \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_t, \nu) \leq s\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu) + \tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_s, \mu_t) +(1-t)\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)
    \]
    Hence $\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu_s, \mu_t) \geq (t-s)\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_p(\mu, \nu)$ and equality follows.
\end{proof}

In practice the Monge problem is solved via numerical algorithms such as the 2nd-order PDE Monge-Ampère Equation.

\begin{definition}[Monge-Ampère Equation]
    Given a domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and some function $f$ the Monge-Ampère equation is given by
    \[
    \det D^2 u = f(x), \ u: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{{R}}
    \]
    The PDE is often solved numerically (E.g. Bellmann Equations).
\end{definition}

In fact, the change of variables from Theorem 1.12 gives us the Monge-Ampère Equation
\begin{proposition}[Monge-Ampère for Brenier's Theorem]
    \[
\det (\nabla^2 \psi(x) \rho_\nu (\nabla \psi(x)) = \rho_\mu(x).
\]
\end{proposition}


This gives way to Caffarelli regularity which explores when optimal transport maps are smooth deformations rather than an a.e. gradient.
To solve 1.22 numerically it is possible to linearize the problem to a weighted Poisson equation.

\begin{proposition}[Linearization of Monge-Ampère]
    Let $\rho_\varepsilon = \rho_0 + \varepsilon r + o(\varepsilon)$ be a smooth perburation of a positive reference desntiy $\rho_0$ on a smooth bounded domain with $\int r = 0$. If $T_\varepsilon(x) = x+ \varepsilon\nabla u(x) + o(\varepsilon)$ transports $\rho_0$ to $\rho_\varepsilon$ then 
    \[
    - \nabla \cdot (\rho_0 \nabla u) = r.
    \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The change of variables equation for $T_\varepsilon$ is given by 
    \[
    \rho_0(x) = \rho_\varepsilon(T_\varepsilon(x))\det(\nabla T_\varepsilon(x)),
    \]
    Doing a Taylor expansion on $\rho_\varepsilon(x + \varepsilon \nabla u) = \rho_0(x) + \varepsilon r(x) + \varepsilon\langle\nabla \rho_0, \nabla u \rangle + o(\varepsilon)$ such that 
    \[
    \det(I + \varepsilon \nabla ^2 u) = 1 + \varepsilon \Delta u + o(\varepsilon)
    \]
    Therefore $\rho_0 = \rho_0 + \varepsilon(r + \langle \nabla \rho_0, \nabla u \rangle + \rho_0 \Delta u) + o(\varepsilon) = \rho + \varepsilon(r + \nabla \cdot (\rho_0 \nabla u)) + o(\varepsilon)$ and the first order terms vanish.
\end{proof}

\subsection{1D Transport and Quantiles}
\begin{definition}[Cumulative and Quantile Functions]
    Let $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(\mathbb{R})$ the cumulative distribution function and generalized inverse / quantile function are denoted
    \[
    \mathcal{C}_\mu(x) = \mu((-\infty, x])
    \]
    \[
    \mathcal{C}_\mu^{-1}(p) = \inf\{x \in \mathbb{R}: \mathcal{C}_\mu(x) \geq p\}
    \]
    Respectively, where $p \in (0, 1)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Quantile Push-Forward]
    $(\mathcal{C}_\mu^{-1})_\#(\lambda_{[0, 1]}) = \mu$ where $\lambda$ denotes Lebesgue measure / Uniform distribution on $[0, 1]$. If $\mu$ is atomless then $(\mathcal{C}_\mu)_\#\mu = \lambda_{[0, 1]}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    WLOG assume $\mu$ has strictly positive density such that $\mathcal{C}_\alpha$ is strictly increasing and continuous, and let $\gamma = (\mathcal{C}_\mu^{-1})_\#\lambda_{[0, 1]}$, it suffices to prove that $\mathcal{C}_\gamma = \mathcal{C}_\mu$. Then
    \[
    \mathcal{C}_\gamma(x) = \displaystyle \int_0^1 \mathds{1}_{(-\infty, x]}(\mathcal{C}_\mu^{-1}(z))dz = \int_0^1 \mathds{1}_{[0, \mathcal{C}_\mu(x)]}(z)dz = \mathcal{C}_\mu(x)
    \]
    By using $\mathcal{C}_\mu^{-1}(z) \leq x$ iff $z \leq \mathcal{C}_\mu(x)$. If $\mu$ is atomless, the last claim follows from the Integral Probability Transform.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    If $\mu$ is atomless then $T = \mathcal{C}_\nu^{-1} \circ \mathcal{C}_\mu$ is the Monge Optimal Transport map in 1D via Brenier's theorem when $c(x, y) = \lvert x - y \rvert^2$. An analogous closed-form solution for the 1D Kantorovich case will be discussed in Section 2. In fact, optimal transport maps compose for probability measures $\mu, \gamma, \nu$ such that $T_{\mu \rightarrow \gamma} = \mathcal{C}_\gamma^{-1} \circ \mathcal{C}_\mu, \ T_{\gamma \rightarrow \nu} = \mathcal{C}_\nu^{-1} \circ \mathcal{C}_\gamma$ then $T_{\mu \rightarrow \nu} = T_{\gamma \rightarrow \nu} \circ T_{\mu \rightarrow \gamma}$ satisfies the push-foward condition and is well-defined almost everywhere; this is a unique example of the gluing lemma.
\end{remark}

The Monge 1D closed-form solution $T$ for continuous measures does not hold for discrete measures / when atoms exist and is generalized in the Kantorovich setting to couplings instead of maps.

An alternative algorithm to build multivariate transport maps is via the Knothe-Rosenblatt triangular rearrangement, which in general does not return the candidate map but instead transports particles along each axis sequentially. 

\begin{proposition}[Knothe-Rosenblatt Triangular Rearrangement]
    Let $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(\mathbb{R}^d)$, assume that the first marginal of $\mu$ is atomless along with its 1D conditional laws, then there is a triangular map
    \[
    T(x_1, ..., x_d) = (T_1(x_1), T_2(x_1, x_2), ..., T_d(x_1, ..., x_d))
    \]
    Such that $T_\#\mu = \nu$ and for each $k$ $x_k \rightarrow T_k$ is non-decreasing $\mu$-almost every value of $x_1, ..., x_{k-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The proof follows from recursive construction; assume for $k = 1$ let $T_1$ be the monotone rearrangement between the first marginal of $\mu$ and $\nu$ respectively. Then suppose $T_1, ..., T_{k - 1}$ have already been constructed; denote $x_{<k} = (x_1, ..., x_{k - 1}), T_{< k} = (T_1, ..., T_{k - 1})$ and by induction $(T_{<k})_\# \mu_{<k} = \nu_{<k}$ pushing the first $k-1$ marginals. Define $T_k(x_{<k}, \ . \ )$ which is the 1D monotone rearrangement from the regular conditional laws of the $k$-th coordinate given to the previous coordinates of $\mu$ and $\nu$ respectively, which is non-decreasing and via chain rule, at step $k$ we get that the first $k$ coordinates of $T_\# \mu$ have the same conditional law as $\nu$ which converges in $d$ steps.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gaussian Measures and the Bures Metric}

Gaussians are arguably the most important family of distributions; optimal transport gives us geometric properties of its parameters.

\begin{proposition}[Transport of 1D Gaussians]
    Let $\mu \sim N(m_\mu, \sigma_\mu^2)$ and $\nu \sim N(m_\nu, \sigma_\nu^2)$ be two non-degenerate Gaussian distributions on $\mathbb{R}$, then
    \[
    T(x) = \frac{\sigma_\nu}{\sigma_\mu}(x - m_\mu) + m_\nu
    \]
    is the optimal transport map and satisfies the push-forward constraint, and under Brenier's Theorem, $T = \nabla \psi$ where $\psi(x) = \frac{\sigma_\nu}{2\sigma_\mu} (x - m_\mu)^2 + m_\nu x$ and the Monge distance follows as 
    \[
    \mathcal{W}_2(\mu, \nu)^2 = \displaystyle \int_\mathbb{R} (\frac{\sigma_\nu}{\sigma_\mu}(x - m_\mu) + m_\nu - x)^2 d\mu(x) = (m_\mu - m_\nu)^2 + (\sigma_\mu - \sigma_\nu)^2.
    \]
    It cleanly follows that the squared Monge-2 distance is the Euclidean metric across $(\mu, \sigma) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ space.
\end{proposition}

We will show that the Monge distance for MVN distributions is not Euclidean wrt mean vectors and covariance matrices.

\begin{proposition}[Transport of Multivariate Gaussians]
    Let $\mu \sim N(m_\mu, \Sigma_\mu)$, $\nu \sim N(m_\nu, \Sigma_\nu)$ and $T(x) = m_\nu + A(x - m_\mu)$, then $T = \nabla \psi$ where $\psi(x) = m_\nu^T x + \frac{(x-m_\mu)^T A(x - m_\mu)}{2}$ iff $A$ is symmetric PSD.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Affine Push-Forward of Gaussians]
    $T_\# \mu = \nu$ iff $A \Sigma_\mu A^T = \Sigma_\nu$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    $T(\mu) \sim N(m_\nu + A(m_\mu - m_\mu), A\Sigma_\mu A^T)$ equal to $\nu$ iff $A\Sigma_\mu A^T = \Sigma_\nu$.
\end{proof}

Under symmetry of Brenier's Theorem we have $A\Sigma_\mu A = \Sigma_\nu$. We promptly show that the covariance term in the Monge distance formula is given by the Bures metric.

\begin{definition}[Bures Metric]
    For PSD covariance matrices $\Sigma$, $\Lambda$ the Bures metric is defined by $\mathcal{B}(\Sigma, \Lambda)^2 = \text{tr}(\Sigma + \Lambda - 2(\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} \Lambda \Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}})$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Bures Covariance of Gaussian $\mathcal{W}_2$]
    Assume $\Sigma_\mu, \Sigma_\nu$ are PD, then the unique PD solution of $A\Sigma_\mu A = \Sigma_\nu$ is given by $A = \Sigma_\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}} (\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2} \Sigma_\nu \Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2})^\frac{1}{2} \Sigma_\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}$. Then 
    \[
    \mathcal{W}_2(\mu, \nu)^2 = \lvert m_\mu - m_\nu \rvert^2 + \mathcal{B}(\Sigma_\mu, \Sigma_\nu)^2.
    \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    For the first claim multiply both sides by $\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2}$ gives $(\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2} A \Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2})^2 = \Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2}A\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2}$ and $\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2}A\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2}$ is symmetric PD and the unique positive square root on RHS, and the identity of $A$ above is also unique therefore the expressions match.
    \newline

    For the second statement, notice that $T$ is optimal under Brenier; then
    \[
    \mathbb{E}\lvert X - T(X) \rvert^2 = \lvert m_\mu - m_\nu \rvert^2 + \mathbb{E} \lvert(I - A)(X - m_\mu) \rvert^2 = \lvert m_\mu - m_\nu \rvert^2 + \text{tr}((I - A)\Sigma_\mu(I - A)^T)
    \]
    \[
    = \lvert m_\mu - m_\nu \rvert^2 + \text{tr}(\Sigma_\mu) + \text{tr}(A \Sigma_\mu A) - 2\text{tr}(A\Sigma_\mu) = \lvert m_\mu - m_\nu \rvert^2 + \text{tr}(\Sigma_\mu) + \text{tr}(\Sigma_\nu) - 2\text{tr}((\Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2} \Sigma_\nu \Sigma_\mu^\frac{1}{2})^\frac{1}{2})
    \]
    The remaining terms precisely defines the Bures metric.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Metric and Convexity of the Bures term]
    The function $\mathcal{B}$ from Proposition 1.33 is a distance function for PSD covariance matrices. Futhermore, $\mathcal{B}^2$ is jointly convex such that for $t \in [0, 1]$,
    \[
    \mathcal{B}^2((1-t)\Sigma_0 + t\Sigma_1, (1-t)\Lambda_0 + t\Lambda_1) \leq (1-t)\mathcal{B}^2(\Sigma_0, \Lambda_0) + t\mathcal{B}^2(\Sigma_1, \Lambda_1).
    \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Use the Proscustes representation 
    \[
    \mathcal{B}^2(\Sigma, \Lambda) = \min_{Q^T Q = I} \lvert \Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} - \Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q \rvert_F^2 = \text{tr}(\Sigma) + \text{tr}(\Lambda) - 2\text{tr}((\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\Lambda \Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}})^\frac{1}{2}).
    \]
    Symmetry, positivity and separation follow immediately.
    \newline
    
    For the triangle inequality, choose almost optimal orthogonal matrices $Q_1, Q_2$ such that
    \[
    \lvert \Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} - \Gamma^{\frac{1}{2}}Q_2Q_1 \rvert_F \leq \lvert \Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} - \Lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}Q_1 \rvert_F + \lvert \Lambda^\frac{1}{2}Q_2 \rvert_F
    \]
    Setting the matrices to be optimal proves the metric property.

    \newline
    To prove convexity, use the equivalent factor formulation $\mathcal{B}^2(\Sigma, \Lambda) = \min_{UU^T = \Sigma, VV^T = \Lambda} \rvert U - V \rvert_F^2$ and once again choose almost optimal factors $(U_0, V_0)$ and $(U_1, V_1)$, then define block factors 
    \[
    U_t = [\sqrt{1-t}U_0, \sqrt{t}U_1], \ V_t = [\sqrt{1-t}V_0, \sqrt{t}V_1]
    \]
    Then $U_tU_t^T = (1-t)\Sigma_0 + t\Sigma_1$ and $V_tV_t^T = (1-t)\Lambda_0 + t\Lambda_1$ therefore
    \[
    \lvert U_t - V_t \rvert_F^2 = (1-t) \lvert U_0 - V_0 \rvert_F^2 + t \lvert U_1 - V_1 \rvert_F^2.
    \]
    Taking the infimum proves joint convexity.
\end{proof}

\section{Kantorovich Formulation}
150 years after the Monge problem, Kantorovich found a way to relax optimal transport maps into couplings and allow for distributional mass splitting.

\subsection{Relaxation of Probability Measures}

Recall the lack of mass splitting in the Monge formulation implies there may be no transport map $T$ that exists between measures. Furthermore, Monge maps may be asymmetric; a transport plan may exist from a atomless distribution to an atomic one but not vice versa. We will show that there always exists a Kantorovich solution but also improved tractability for numerical algorithms. 

\begin{definition}[Marginals of joint measure]
    Let $\pi \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X \times Y)$ and $P_X, P_Y$ be the respective coordinate projection maps, then the marginals of $\pi$ are
    \[
    \pi_1 = (P_X)_\# \pi \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X), \ \pi_2 = (P_Y)_\# \pi \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(Y).
    \]
    Equivalently for all test functions $f$ on $X$ and $g$ on $Y$ we have
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_{X \times Y} f(x)d\pi(x, y) = \int_X f d\pi_1, \ \int_{X \times Y}g(y)d\pi(x, y) = \int_Y g d\pi_2.
    \]
\end{definition}


{\setlength{\parindent}{0cm}
The marginal constraints of the coupling are given by $(P_X)_\#\pi = \mu, \ (P_Y)_\#\pi = \nu$ or $\pi(A \times Y) = \mu(A), \ \pi(X \times B) = \nu(B)$ for measurable sets.

\begin{definition}[Couplings]
    Given probability measures $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X), \ \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(Y)$, the set of couplings between $\mu$ and $\nu$ is denoted as
    \[
    \Gamma(\mu, \nu) = \{\pi \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X \times Y):  \pi_1 = \mu, \ \pi_2 = \nu\}.
    \]
\end{definition}

Unlike the Monge push-forward constraint, the feasible set is never empty, given by the tensor product / product coupling.

\begin{definition}[Tensor Product / Trivial Coupling]
    Given $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X), \ \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(Y)$, the tensor coupling $\mu \otimes \nu$ is the probability measure on $X \times Y$ defined by
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_{X \times Y} h(x, y)d(\mu \otimes \nu)(x, y) = \int_X \bigg (\int_Y h(x, y)d\nu(y)\bigg)d\mu(x)
    \]
    for all bounded measurable $h$.
\end{definition}

Even though the feasibility set is non-empty, the tensor coupling is almost never optimal in practice.

\newpage

\begin{proposition}[Degeneracy of Tensor Coupling]
    Assume $X, Y$ are compact metric spaces and $c \in \mathcal{C}(X \times Y)$; the following statements are equivalent;
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\mu \otimes \nu = \text{argmin}_{\pi \in \Gamma(\mu, \nu)} \displaystyle \int c \ d\pi,$
        \item Every feasible coupling is optimal,
        \item $c(x, y) = u(x) + v(y)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    We show that for $x_0, x_1 \in \text{supp}(\mu)$ and $y_0, y_1 \in \text{supp}(\nu)$ then \[
    c(x_0, y_0) + c(x_1, y_1) = c(x_1, y_0) + c(x_0, y_1).
    \]
    Suppose by contradiction equality fails such that (rearranging $y_0, y_1$ if necessary)
    \[
    c(x_0, y_0) + c(x_1, y_1) > c(x_1, y_0) + c(x_0, y_1)
    \]
    and by continuity this holds true for small neighborhoods $U_0, U_1$ for $x_0, x_1$ and $V_0, V_1$ for $y_0, y_1$ respectively. Then these neighborhoods have positive marginal mass as the four points lie in the support of the respective measures. Denote $\mu_i, \nu_i$ the respective measures with domains restricted to $U_i, V_i$, and choose $\lambda$ such that $0 \leq \lambda \leq \min\{\mu(U_0)\nu(V_0), \mu(U_1)\nu(V_1)\}$. Then the exchanged measure 
    \[
    \tilde{\pi} = \mu \otimes \nu - \lambda (\mu_0 \otimes \nu_0) - \lambda (\mu_1 \otimes \nu_1) + \lambda(\mu_0 \otimes \nu_1) + \lambda(\mu_1 \otimes \nu_0)
    \]
    is non-negative and has the same marginals as $\mu \otimes \nu$. Then the strict equality on the neighborhoods imply that $\displaystyle \int c \ d\tilde{\pi} < \int c\ d(\mu \otimes \nu)$ arriving at a contradiction. Therefore $(1) \rightarrow (2)$ and the reverse implication follows trivially.
    \bigskip
    
    Then fixing $x^* \in \text{supp}(\mu), y^* \in \text{supp}(\nu)$ and using the equality above we get $c(x, y) = c(x, y^*) + c(x^*, y) - c(x^*, y^*)$ thus $c = u + v$ on the product support. Every coupling is concentrated on said support and $\displaystyle \int c \ d\pi = \int u \ d\mu + \int v \ d\nu$ only depends on the marginals, hence all couplings are optimal.

    
\end{proof}

We can now define a rigorous formulation of the Kantorovich Problem;
\begin{definition}[Kantorovich Problem]
        Given $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X), \ \nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(Y)$ and cost function $c: X \times Y \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, Kantorovich's problem is formulated as
        \[
        \mathcal{L}_c(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Gamma(\mu, \nu)} \displaystyle \int_{X \times Y}c(x, y)d\pi(x, y).
        \]
        An equivalent formulation can be defined as
        \[
        \mathcal{L}_c(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{U \sim \mu, V \sim \nu}\mathbb{E}(c(U, V))
        \]
        where $U, V$ are random variables.
    \end{definition}

Unlike Monge's problem, Kantorovich achieves an optimum under fairly weak regularity conditions.

\begin{proposition}[Existence of Kantorovich Solution(s) under Compact Spaces]
    Assume $X, Y$ are compact metric spaces and $c \in \mathcal{C}(X \times Y)$, then the Kantorovich problem admits a minimizer.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Non-emptiness of constraint set has been proven; $X \times Y$ is compact hence Polish, then by Prokhorov's Theorem the set of all probability measures on $X \times Y$ is tight thus compact (Any probability measure on a Polish space is tight). To show that the constraint set is closed via weak convergence, let $\{\pi_n\} \subset \Gamma(\mu, \nu)$ such that $\pi_n \rightarrow \pi$ weakly, pick $f \in \mathcal{C}(X)$ and take the marginal projection $p_X: X \times Y \rightarrow X$, compose to get $f \circ p_X \in \mathcal{C}(X \times Y)$ such that with the push-forward definition, $(p_X)_\#\pi_n = \mu$ so
    \[
    \displaystyle \int_X f d\mu = \int_X f d[(p_X)_\#\pi_n] = \int_{X \times Y} (f\circ p_X) d\pi_n \rightarrow \int_{X \times Y} (f \circ p_X) d\pi = \int_X f d[(p_X)_\#\pi] = \int_X f d\mu
    \]
    Hence $(p_X)_\#\pi = \mu$ and repeat the argument to get $(p_Y)_\#\pi = \nu$ therefore the product is closed, hence the constraint set is closed and compact.

    Finally, the $c$ is bounded (Since $c$ is continuous on a compact set), the functional $J(\pi_n) =  \displaystyle \int c \ d\pi_n \rightarrow \int c \ d\pi = J(\pi)$ is weakly continuous, thus the infimum is attained on the compact constraint set, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

We take a detour back to Monge's formulation and realize that the induced Kantorovich plan admits the same minimizer under Brenier's theorem.

\begin{corollary}[Induced Kantorovich via Monge]
    Let $\mu$ be absolutely continuous wrt Lebesgue and $c(x, y) = \lvert x - y \rvert^2$, if $T$ is the Brenier map solving Monge, then $\pi = (\text{Id}, T)_\#\mu$ solves the Kantorovich problem.
\end{corollary}

Kantorovich's formulation mathematically allows for mass splitting as Definition 2.3 yields non-triviality of the constraint set, and Proposition 2.6 relaxes the problem to arbitrary measures (Assuming compact restricted domains), which bypasses the huge bottleneck in Monge-style problems. 

\begin{example}
    Recall that for the counterexample with $\mu \sim \delta_0$ (WLOG $X = \{0\}$) and $\nu \sim N(0, 1)$ yields no Monge solution; under Kantorovich's formulation the optimal coupling becomes $\pi = \delta_1 \otimes N(0, 1)$ with optimal loss
    \[
    \mathcal{L}_c(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\pi \in \Gamma(\mu, \nu)} \displaystyle \int_{X \times \mathbb{R}} \lvert x - y \rvert^2 d\pi(x, y) = \int_{X \times \mathbb{R}} \lvert x - y \rvert^2 \delta_0(dx)\nu(dy) = \int_\mathbb{R} \lvert 0 - y \rvert^2 \phi(y)dy = \mathbb{E}(Y^2) = 1.
    \]

    Where $\phi$ is the pdf of the standard normal distribution. Furthermore, the optimal coupling is unique (This does not hold true in general). \hfill \(\square\)

\end{example}

\subsection{Discrete Relaxation}
Kantorovich's formulation yields a solution for discrete measures when $n \neq m$ and the transport coupling becomes a matrix $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}_+$ such that $P_{ij}$ describes the amount of mass moved from $x_i$ to $y_j$ where $\mu = \sum_{i = 1}^n a_i \delta_{x_i}$ and $\nu = \sum_{j = 1}^m b_j \delta_{y_j}$.

\begin{definition}[Couplings]
    The set of admissible couplings between discrete measures $\mu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(X)$ and $\nu \in \mathcal{M}_1^+(Y)$ given above is defined by
    \[
    U(a, b) = \{P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}_+; P \mathds{1}_m = a, P^T \mathds{1}_n = b \}.
    \]
\end{definition}

Analogous to the continuous case, the constraint set is always non-empty (Due to the trivial coupling) and the optimality of the product coupling is degenerate.

\begin{definition}[Discrete Product Coupling]
    Given weights $a \in \Sigma_n$ and $b \in \Sigma_m$, the trivial coupling is given by $(a \otimes b)_{i, j} = a_i b_j$.
\end{definition}

\begin{corollary}[Product Coupling Optimality is Degenerate]
    Remove all zero rows and columns such that $a_j, b_j > 0$ and $C_{ij} = c(x_i, y_j)$ is a finite cost matrix. Then $(a \otimes b)$ minimizes $P \rightarrow \langle C, P \rangle = \sum_{i = 1}^n \sum_{j = 1}^m P_{ij}C_{ij}$ subject to $P \in U(a, b)$ iff every coupling in $U(a, b)$ minimizes it.
\end{corollary}

Kantorovich invented the first form of linear programming such that the transport cost is linear in the amount of transported mass, with the discrete formulation below;

\begin{definition}[Discrete Kantorovich Problem]
    For $a \in \Sigma_n, b \in \Sigma_m \ (\mu = a^T\delta_x,  \nu = b^T \delta_y \ \text{via abuse of notation})$ and $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, define
    \[
    L_C(a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a, b)} \langle C, P \rangle = \min_{P \in U(a, b)} \sum_{i, j} C_{i,j} P_{i,j}.
    \]
    Then the optimal transport plan / coupling $P$ is given in the form of a probabilistic matrix.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Existence of Discrete Kantorovich Solution]
    A solution to Definition 2.11 always exists (Not always unique).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Notice that the constraint set $U(a, b)$ is compact by Heine-Borel (Since $P \in U(a, b)$ is bounded by $0 \leq P_{ij} \leq 1$ and for any sequence $P_k$ the entries converge pointwise so $U(a, b)$ is sequentially closed), since the program is linear in $P$ then the minimum exists by EVT.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Convexity / Concavity properties]
    For fixed cost function $C$, the Kantorovich problem $L_C$ is convex wrt simplexes. For fixed simplexes $a, b$, $L_C$ is concave wrt cost matrix $C$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    For $t \in [0, 1]$ and $(a_0, b_0), (a_1, b_1) \in \Sigma_n \times \Sigma_m$ with optimal matrices $P_0, P_1$ we have $tP_0 + (1-t)P_1 \in U(t(a_0, b_0) + (1-t)(a_1, b_1))$ so
    \[
    L_C(t(a_0, b_0) + (1-t)(a_1, b_1)) \leq \langle C, tP_0 + (1-t)P_1 \rangle = tL_C(a_0, b_0) + (1-t)L_C(a_1, b_1)
    \]
    Where the last equality follows from linearity of $L_C$.

    For fixed marginals $a, b$, every $P \in U(a, b)$ satisfies
    \[
    \langle tC_0 + (1-t)C_1, P \rangle \geq tL_{C_0}(a, b) + (1-t)L_{C_1}(a, b)
    \]
    Concavity follows from taking the LHS minimum.
\end{proof}

In fact, the optimal transport matrix for Kantorovich's formulation is sparse for sufficiently large dimensions $n, m$; 

\begin{proposition}[Rank-controlled Sparse Minimizers]
    Let $\mathcal{A}: \mathbb{R}^{n \times m } \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^q$ be linear, $\textbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^q$ and $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, then when $\inf_{P \geq 0, \mathcal{A}(P) \leq \textbf{d}} \langle C, P \rangle$ yields a solution $P^*$, then $P^*$ has at most $\text{rank}(\mathcal{A}) = \dim(\text{Im}(\mathcal{A}))$ non-zero entries.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    See \cite{peyre} Proposition 3.7.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
    Assume $a_i, b_j > 0$ and $\Sigma_i a_i = \Sigma_j b_j = 1$, then the linear program admits an optimal matrix with at most $n + m - 1$ non-zero entries.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The feasible set is non-empty and compact (Recall Proposition 2.6) so the minimum exists and is finite. Then for $\mathcal{A}(P) = (P \mathds{1}_m, P^T \mathds{1}_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n + m}, \ \text{rank}(\mathcal{A}) = n + m - 1$. and Proposition 2.14 completes the proof.
\end{proof}

In practice, libraries such as Python Optimal Transport (POT) uses methods such as the Network Simplex Algorithm for Exact Optimal Transport in $O(N^3)$ time; regularization methods or alternative algorithms will be introduced later for faster computation of transport solutions.

The simple example below demonstrates mass-splitting of the discrete Kantorovich transport plan previously impossible with Monge:

\begin{example}
    Let $\mu = \frac{1}{2}\delta_{-1} + \frac{1}{2}\delta_1 = a^T \delta_x, \ \nu = \frac{1}{6}\delta_{-1} + \frac{1}{3}\delta_0 + \frac{1}{2}\delta_1 = b^T\delta_y$ where $a = (\frac{1}{2} \ \frac{1}{2})^T$ and $b = (\frac{1}{6} \ \frac{1}{3} \ \frac{1}{2})^T$, and $C \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}$ such that $C_{ij} = \lvert x_i - y_j\rvert^2$. Then
    \[
    L_C(a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a, b)} \sum_{ij}C_{ij}P_{ij} = \min_{P \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3}_+: P \mathds{1}_3 = a, P^T \mathds{1}_2 = b} \sum_{ij} C_{ij} P_{ij}
    \]
    and the transport matrix $P^* = \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{1}{6} & \frac{1}{3} & 0\\
        0 & 0 & \frac{1}{2}
    \end{bmatrix}$ minimizes the discrete Kantorovich loss. \hfill \(\square\)
\end{example}

We will now restrict our attention to the special case $n = m$ and uniform weights $a = \frac{1}{n} 1_n, \ b = \frac{1}{m} 1_m$ which optimal transport plans are permutations in the Monge case and remain so under Kantorovich couplings; in fact, the extreme points of the linear programming problem are indeed the Monge permutations; the following results are necessary to show regularity under linear programs instead of continuous probability distributions.

\begin{definition}[Permutation Matrices]
    For a permutation $\sigma \in \text{Perm}(n)$, the permutation matrix $P_\sigma$ is given by $(P_\sigma)_{ij} = 1$ if $j = \sigma(i)$ and 0 otherwise. The set of all permutation matrices is $\mathcal{P}^\text{perm}_n = \{P_\sigma: \sigma \in \text{Perm}(n)\}$ which is non-convex.
\end{definition}

$\mathcal{P}^\text{Perm}_n$ can be made convex by relaxing the set to the finite set of all bistochastic matrices.

\newpage
\begin{definition}[Birkhoff Polytope]
    The Birkhoff polytope is the convex set of all bistochastic matrices \[
    \mathcal{B}_n = \{P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}_+: P \mathds{1}_n = \mathds{1}_n, P^T \mathds{1}_n = \mathds{1}_n  \}.
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Extreme Points]
    For a convex set $\mathcal{C}$ in a finite dimensional vector space, 
    \[
    \text{Extr}(\mathcal{C}) = \{x \in \mathcal{C}: x = \frac{(y + z)}{2}, y, z \in \mathcal{C} \Rightarrow x = y = z\}.
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Existence of Extreme Points]
    If $\mathcal{C} \neq \emptyset$ is a compact convex subset of finite dimensional vector space then Extr$(\mathcal{C}) \neq \emptyset$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Use the stronger result that by Krein-Milman Theorem, any non-empty convex compact $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d \ (d < \infty)$ satisfies that $X$ is equal to the closure of the convex hull of its extreme points; therefore if $\text{Extr(X)} = \emptyset$ then $X = \emptyset$ yields a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Birkhoff-von Neumann]
    The extreme points of $\mathcal{B}_n$ are the permutation matrices.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Invoke Krein-Milman on Mirsky \cite{bvn} Theorem 2. 
\end{proof}

In fact, every transport matrix (which is bistochastic) can be decomposed into a linear combination of permutation matrices.

\begin{corollary}[Birkhoff-von Neumann Decomposition]
    Every $P \in \mathcal{B}_n$ admits a representation
    \[
    P = \sum_{i = r}^L \lambda_r P_{\sigma(r)}, \ \lambda_r > 0, \ \sum_{r = 1}^L \lambda_r = 1, \ L \leq (n - 1)^2 + 1.
    \]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    Let $\mathcal{K}$ be the convex hull of $\mathcal{P}_n^{\text{perm}}$ such that $\mathcal{K} \subset \mathcal{B}_n$. Suppose by contradiction $P \in \mathcal{B}_n \backslash \mathcal{K} \neq \emptyset$ such that strict separation provides a linear form $l$ such that $l(P) < \min_{Q \in \mathcal{K}} l(Q)$; 2.19 and 2.20 yields $\min_{Q \in \mathcal{B}_n} l(Q) = \min_{\sigma \in \text{Perm}(n)} l(P_\sigma) = \min_{Q \in \mathcal{K}} l(Q)$ contradicts $P \in \mathcal{B}_n$ therefore $\mathcal{K} = \mathcal{B}_n$.
    \newline
    \newline
    Then the $n$ rows and $n$ column equations combined have rank $2n - 1$ and $\mathds{1}_n \mathds{1}_n^T / n$ lies in the affine space, so $\mathcal{B}_n$ has dimension $n^2 - (2n - 1) = (n - 1)^2$, then Caratheodory's theorem in the affine hull reduces the convex decomposition to $(n - 1)^2 + 1$ permutation matrices at most, notwithstanding those with zero coefficients. 
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    Two immediate results follow from the decomposition: Firstly, the decomposition need not be unique. Secondly, BvN shows that there does not exist a mass-splitting transport matrix that performs better than permutation matrices in the uniform weights $m = n$ case.
\end{remark}

The BvN decomposition for transport plans have a worst case time complexity of $O(n^5)$ and a memory cost of $O(n^2)$, which can be accelerated by faster bipartite-matching algorithms and/or the residual supports are sparse.
